    Home/Teachers/Learning differences

    Learning differences

    A learning difference is a difference in how a brain takes in, holds onto, or expresses information. It says nothing about intelligence or effort. Here are the differences we see most, how each can look in class, and what helps.

    Good to know

    Differences are specific

    A student can reason at a high level and still find reading, writing, or math genuinely hard. That gap is often the clearest sign.

    They often show up together

    ADHD and anxiety, or ADHD and a reading difference, frequently go together, so one label rarely tells the whole story.

    Some are easy to miss

    A student who is quiet, disorganized, or taking twice as long as everyone else may be working very hard to keep up.

    Anxiety affects memory

    Worry takes up working memory, so an anxious student can know the material and still blank on a test.

    Noticing something in a student?

    This guide is for awareness, not for diagnosing. Please never tell a student or family you think a student might have a specific diagnosis. Those conversations are sensitive and can discourage families from seeking help.

    Tell Learning Services or Counseling what you see, like how long work takes, where the student gets stuck, or what changed. We approach concerns based on evidence and after hearing from several teachers, then guide the student and family through next steps. How to reach out

    Strategies that help across the board

    Some approaches show up again and again below. Building them into everyday teaching supports many students at once.

    Home/Teachers/Accommodations guide

    Accommodations guide

    An accommodation changes how a student accesses material or shows what they know. It does not change what they are expected to learn or the standard they are graded against.

    Why they work

    Every assessment is built to measure a skill. A learning difference can put a barrier between the student and that skill. A timed reading quiz for a student who decodes slowly ends up measuring reading speed instead of understanding. Extended time takes the speed barrier away, so the quiz measures what it was designed to measure.

    That is the test for every accommodation. It removes something that isn’t the skill, so the skill can show. A student with extended time takes the same assessment on the same content.

    What we want to emphasize

    Accommodations are already on
    A student doesn’t need to ask each time, remind a teacher, or produce anything. From the day a plan reaches a teacher, it applies.
    Offered every time
    Students choose whether to use an accommodation in the moment. Teachers make sure it is always available.
    No explanation required
    A student never has to explain their diagnosis to get their accommodations. Plenty of students happily talk about how they learn, and we love that. It is not a condition of access.
    When unsure, provide it
    Give the accommodation and ask us afterward. A plan is easy to adjust. An assessment a student already took without their support is not.
    Standards stay the same
    No one will ever be asked to lower a standard. If an accommodation looks like it would require that, something needs to change, and we want to hear about it.
    Feedback makes plans better
    Teachers see whether an accommodation is doing its job. When something isn’t working or isn’t getting used, telling us is exactly what we want.

    Extended time and projects

    Extended time applies to timed assessments: tests, quizzes, and timed writes. A multi-day project has pacing, not a fixed cutoff, so extended time doesn’t apply to the project as a whole. If a project includes a timed portion, extended time applies to that portion.

    How to run extended time in your classroom

    What a support plan is

    A Student Support Plan describes how a student learns, where their learning difference affects their access to class, and the accommodations that support them. We write each plan from the student’s evaluation and what we see at UHS, then share it with teachers.

    Built from the evaluation
    Evaluators recommend accommodations in their report. Learning Services uses those recommendations, along with what we see at UHS, to write the plan.
    Current, not permanent
    A plan reflects what we know today, and it changes as the student develops.
    Confidential
    A plan goes only to the adults who work directly with the student and need it: teachers, mentor, class dean, counselor if applicable, and learning specialist.
    Where it lives
    Teachers see plans through Student Learning Profiles in Blackbaud.

    Questions about implementation

    When the wording in a plan is unclear, or an accommodation conflicts with the skill being assessed, contact Learning Services before changing or withholding the accommodation.

    Home/Teachers/Teaching strategies

    Teaching strategies that help everyone

    These practices have strong research behind them. They are essential for students with learning differences and good for every student in the room.

    Research-based practices

    One change in your routine supports many students at once.

    Differentiation

    Differentiation means planning around the students in front of you instead of one path for everyone. The goal stays the same for every student. What changes is the route there: how material is presented, how students practice, and how they show what they learned. The idea comes from Carol Ann Tomlinson at the University of Virginia.

    Watch: What is differentiation? (Edutopia)

    Content
    What students work with. Keep the skill the same and vary the entry point and support, like a vocabulary preview before a hard text or practice sets at different levels on the same skill.
    Process
    How students learn it. Present material more than one way, like the textbook, an audiobook, or an interactive activity, and vary grouping: one-on-one, pairs, small groups, or independent work.
    Product
    How students show it. When the format isn’t the skill being assessed, let students choose: a written report, a graphic organizer, an oral explanation, or a model.
    Environment
    Where and how students work. A layout that supports both group and individual work, and a quiet, distraction-free spot for students who need it.

    Strategies to try in any subject

    Tiered assignments

    Everyone practices the same skill, with the task matched to each student’s readiness.

    Choice boards

    Students pick from several activities that all target the skill you chose.

    Compacting

    Students who already know the material show it, then move on to more advanced work.

    Flexible grouping

    Groups change with the activity, skill, or topic, so no one is stuck in one group all year.

    Interest groups

    Students explore the topic through an angle that interests them.

    Learning contracts

    You set the skills and required parts. The student chooses how to complete the work and sets the pace.

    Differentiation takes planning time. Start with one lesson or one strategy, and bring us in. We’re happy to help you plan.

    Ideas by subject

    Math

    • Offer a choice board: a game with a peer, a video, the textbook, or practice problems.
    • Run a mini-lesson for students who didn’t grasp the concept while others move ahead.
    • Use manipulatives for abstract concepts.
    • Ask students who have mastered it to explain their solution step by step.

    Science

    • Set up help stations where students teach each other.
    • Hold a quick Q&A before labs to fill gaps.
    • Build a visual word wall with pictures and labels.
    • Offer the same content as a video, a labeled diagram, and guided notes.

    Reading

    • Use tiered assignments, like a storyboard or a written report.
    • Form reading groups by interest or reading level.
    • Scaffold with anchor charts, diagrams, and reference guides.

    Writing

    • Hold writing conferences from topic choice through editing.
    • Let students choose their topics.
    • Provide graphic organizers and guided notes for outlining.
    • Track writing progress over the year with a checklist.

    English language learners

    • Pair key vocabulary with the student’s home language.
    • Teach the verbs and adjectives of a topic, not just the nouns.
    • Use group work with roles each student can manage.

    Students with learning differences

    • Model skills explicitly, step by step.
    • Use multisensory instruction for reading and spelling.
    • Provide assistive technology, like text-to-speech and speech-to-text.
    • Offer flexible ways to show learning when the format isn’t the skill.

    Differentiation sections adapted in part from Resilient Educator.

    Alternatives to cold calling

    Cold calling can shut out students who understand the material. Students with auditory processing differences may need extra time to process a question and form an answer, even when their understanding is intact. Students with anxiety may freeze when put on the spot, even when they know the answer.

    Start with the skill you want to check, then pick a way to see it that doesn’t depend on an instant answer.

    • Have students discuss the question in small groups first.
    • Give everyone time to think and write a response before anyone shares.
    • Let students know ahead of time that you’ll call on everyone, so they can prepare.
    • Check in one-on-one.

    Gamification

    Quick, low-stakes game quizzes check understanding without cold calling. Students get instant feedback, and the results show you who needs more support before the next lesson.

    Home/Teachers/Testing in your classroom

    Extended time in your classroom

    Most students with accommodations test in your room. Here is how to run that block so extended time actually works as extended time.

    The core idea

    Extended time only means something if the test is shorter for everyone else. If a test fills the whole block, every student uses the whole block, and the students with extended time get nothing extra. Write the base test shorter than the block.

    How the block runs

    The two bars start together and end differently. That gap is the accommodation. If the test filled the whole block, the dashed box on the standard time lane wouldn’t exist and extended time would be meaningless.

    At 40 minutes, everyone takes a two-minute break at the same time, so no one is the only student getting up and no one is the only student staying seated. Then standard-time students turn in and leave, and extended-time students return to finish.

    These numbers are an example, not a rule. Your assessment might run long, short, in sections, or across a lab. What matters is the gap. One check that helps: students generally need about three times as long as you do on your own test.

    The class waits

    No new material until everyone has finished. Hold discussion, the next unit, and going over the test until every student has turned it in. If the class moves on, students with extended time fall behind on the material, and the accommodation costs them something instead of giving them something.

    Quizzes and shorter assessments

    Extended time applies to quizzes too. Nobody leaves, so standard-time students stay in the room while extended-time students finish. Give them something quiet and non-curricular, like a word search, a puzzle, or a book.

    Here the quiz is 20 minutes and extended time adds 10, so the class waits 10 minutes before moving on. Plan the quiz so that wait fits inside your block.

    Keeping it low-key

    Collect and dismiss without narrating who is staying or why. No announcement, no names, no mention of plans or accommodations. If someone asks why a classmate is still working, students are at different points and a test is not a race.

    Who tests in the Learning Center instead

    Students with 100% extended time, a separate setting, a reader, a scribe, or a computer for handwritten work, plus any student who has submitted the request form for that assessment.

    When a student requests a test in the Learning Center, you get an email with everything you need. Here’s what to do next.

    When your student emails a teacher or comes to see us on their own, that is the goal working.

    Tell us when something isn’t working

    Plans are easy to adjust, and the sooner we hear, the sooner it changes.

    Looking ahead to college

    Accommodations don’t transfer automatically
    At college, your student, not you, contacts the disability services office, submits documentation that meets that college’s requirements, and requests accommodations. No one there will notice a need and reach out.
    Documentation ages
    Colleges often want an evaluation from within a certain window, so one that carried your student through high school may not be accepted. Ask us in eleventh grade whether your student’s documentation will still hold up.
    Plan early for the SAT and ACT
    The College Board and ACT each review accommodations requests on their own schedule. Talk with us well before a test date.

    Home/Learning Center

    Testing in the Learning Center

    The Learning Center is in M-313 on Middle Campus. Students request a test with one short form, and the teacher gets an email with everything they need to send it to us.

    Request a test

    Two school days

    Fill out the form at least two school days before your test. Tell your teacher first so they know you’re testing with us.

    Open the request form

    1. Type your full name

      Your school email is recorded automatically.

    2. Pick your subject, class, and teacher

      Then click Next.

    3. Choose the date and block

      Use the same date your class is testing, then pick the block you’ll take it in.

    4. Answer the makeup question

      If it’s a makeup exam, confirm the date with your teacher before you submit.

    5. Submit

      That’s it. Your teacher gets an email right away.

    On test day

    These agreements keep the Learning Center a calm place to test.

    • Arrive on time. Students who are late will need to reschedule.
    • Put your belongings in the Learning Center office before testing begins.
    • The proctor keeps the time.
    • Your teacher approves all exam materials.
    • The Learning Center provides assistive technology.
    • The Learning Center provides scratch paper.
    • Water only.
    • Breaks follow your plan: one or two 5-minute breaks, like a walk down the hall and back. Test time keeps running.
    • Keep the room a productive place for everyone testing.
